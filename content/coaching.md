# How we coach

How sessions are structured and run, the skills pyramid we build through, and the standing rules about contact. **This is the shared default.** To make it your own, copy this file into your team folder and edit it there — from then on your team uses your copy and stops inheriting changes to this one.

Read it alongside `age-group.md` (your squad's context, training days and facilities), which is always the team's own.

## How sessions should be coached

- Sessions run in **6-week themed blocks**.
- **Every session should map back to how you say you want to play.** When designing one, be able to name which of the guiding principles in `playbook.md` the session serves. A drill that maps to nothing is a drill you don't need.
- **Whole–part–whole applies within every single session, not just across a block.** Each session should bookend a short, focused skill fix with game-play. **Game-zone and skill-zone can be structured in any of three ways, whichever suits that session best:** run them sequentially (game, then a short skill fix, then back into the game — or skill fix first, then game); run the skill zone **in parallel** as a pull-out from the game, with a coach rotating small groups out for their fix and straight back in while the game keeps going for everyone else (needs at least two coaches to split game/skill-zone duty); or use a brief **whole-group coach intervention** — a coaching point delivered to everyone before the game starts, for something every player needs to hear once, as distinct from a specialist-only skill zone run separately. None of the three is the default; pick whichever fits the content and the group sizes. Don't front-load a block with pure skill work and save the games for later weeks — every session, from week 1, needs strong game-play, however the skill zone is structured around it.
- **Skill zones should be short and sharp, not long isolated drilling.** Keep skill-zone time tight — a matter of minutes, high-rep, clearly tied to a specific fix — whichever of the three shapes is used. **Five minutes at a pop is the pull-out length that works:** out of the game, the fix, straight back in, returning later for another five, rather than one long block away from everyone else.
- **Sessions should be very active and fun.** No boring coach-talking time, no standing in queues, no long unbroken drilling — games and adaptive/conditioned play should be the majority of session time, with a pace that keeps players moving and genuinely enjoying themselves, not just physically active. Use conditions and rule tweaks on the game to shape practice of the target skill (a scoring bonus for using it, a constraint that forces it) rather than relying on repetitive isolated drills to teach it.
- **This is also how you get fit.** Every session should carry a **high cardiovascular load through constant activity that looks like a game** — sustained involvement, short resets, quick transitions. **No shuttles, no laps, no conditioning for its own sake.** If players are standing still the session is failing twice, on the skill and on the fitness.
- **Build week to week, with progression.** Sessions within a block should reuse and build on the same activities rather than starting fresh each week — the same game returns with the constraint tightened, the space reduced, or the next layer of the skill added, so players get repetition without it feeling repetitive.
- **Less time explaining, more time doing.** Coach explanations should be as short as possible; prioritise players getting reps in over coaches talking.
- **Coach inside the game wherever the game will carry it.** A whole-group intervention is for something every player needs to hear once and can't pick up from playing; most things are not that. **Prefer the non-intrusive intervention: coach into the stoppages the game already has** — a lineout, a restart, a team walking back — and to one or two players at a time, rather than stopping fifteen of them to talk. It costs no session time at all, and it lands while the picture is still in front of them. **Don't stack interventions back to back**; two blocks of standing still in a row is a lecture however short each one was. Freezing a moving game is a last resort, not a first.
- **Spot-coach the worst two or three out of a drill that is already running.** One coach watches for poor form and a spare coach takes those players aside for a couple of minutes while the drill keeps going for everyone else. **Take them out in pairs rather than singly** wherever the numbers allow, so nobody feels singled out. It fixes the individual without stopping the group, and it costs nobody any reps. Spare coaches coach on the run through these drills rather than watching them — with one exception: **a coach deliberately standing back and observing is a job, not a spare body.** Nobody running an activity can see what the coach with nothing in their hands can.
- **Whoever is coordinating the session should not also be the one starting an activity.** Hand the drill to another coach and keep the coordinating hands free — otherwise the activity that gets least coaching is the one running while the coordinator is looking at the next thing.
- **Demonstrate with players, not coaches, wherever you can.** Pick a player (or a pair) out of the group, brief them in a few words, and let the squad watch them do it. A coach demo shows an adult body at adult speed doing something a child then has to translate; a player demo shows the thing at the size and pace they will actually meet it, and it tells you immediately whether the instruction was understood — if the demo player has it wrong, the explanation was wrong. Pick the demonstrator on purpose: someone who will get it roughly right is the aim, not always the same confident player, and never someone who would hate being watched. Rotate it around the squad over a block. Coaches still demo where a player genuinely can't — an unsafe or unfamiliar contact position, or where the coach is standing in as opposition rather than modelling the skill.
- **Transitions and water breaks are session time, so plan them.** Put the water breaks in the run-sheet — on the back of a break the squad already gets, like a team swap at half time — rather than calling them ad hoc, and start each new activity fast: the markings out in advance, the groups known, the brief in a sentence. Stop/start between blocks is where a session quietly loses ten minutes.
- **Every session includes passing, tackling, or both.** They are the foundations everything else rests on, and they run through the whole season rather than being covered in a block and left behind.
- **While the ground is hard, tackle work needs mats** — or the softest ground available. Check they can be got hold of before planning a tackle-heavy session, and drop the volume rather than tackle onto bare ground.
- **Always run the two-minute contact warm-up before any contact.** See Contact warm-up below. It applies to every piece of contact work, closed skills included — tackle technique, scrum engage, contact games. The player-led warm-up (see `warmup.md`) finishes on the pads, which warms the body for contact — but a shield is not a tackle, so these two minutes still run before any tackling.
- **One voice per segment.** Whenever the whole group is being coached at once, **one coach talks and the others hold**. Enthusiasm is not the problem — four or five constructive side-bar conversations during the same segment is, because the group stops knowing who to listen to. Predictable, single-source instruction also keeps the cognitive load down for neurodiverse players.
- **Every segment has one objective, and you stick to it.** If the segment is team lineout positioning, it is not the moment to fix an individual's throwing action, however tempting. Note it, and take it to that player in the next skill zone or at the end. Coaches should know each segment's objective before the session starts. **The session's own focus is the harder one to hold** — an attacking move run inside a defence-focused session pulls every coach's eye onto the attack. Say the session focus out loud to the coaching team at the start, and again at each segment.
- **Pick the session's two teams at the warm-up, and split along that line all session.** Two roughly equal groups, each with a sensible number of forwards and backs, **one side in bibs or headbands** — done while the players are warming up, so it costs no session time. Everything built on the teams then splits the same way — the forwards packs, a team run-through, the game itself — so no segment starts with two minutes of picking sides. A handling drill that only needs two equal groups is not one of them; split that however suits the space. **For small-sided games, halve each team rather than picking again** — half the bibbed team against half the unbibbed one, twice over, so every game still has a bibbed side against an unbibbed one and two colours cover four teams.
- **Agree who is doing what before the session.** Assign roles in the few minutes before the start, or during the player-led warm-up — that window works well and costs nothing. With several coaches out, unassigned is the same as everyone doing a bit of everything. **Do the rest of it on the coaches' thread, not on the grass** — share the starting setup ahead of time, so it can go out while people are still arriving, and call out anything likely to generate a coaching discussion beforehand. The discussion is worth having; it is just worth having before the players are standing there.

## Contact warm-up

**Two minutes, every time, before anyone makes contact.** Three stages, always in this order, never skipping ahead: get the body into position, then two contact games that get hands on an opponent and the heart rate up. Games rather than drills — two minutes of queuing to hit a pad prepares nobody, and these get every player working for the whole two minutes.

| Stage | Time | What |
|---|---|---|
| 1. **Get low** | ~30s | On the spot. Feet moving, then drop into the tackle-ready position — feet shoulder-width, knees bent, chest up, eyes up, hands ready. Five or six reps. Cue: **low**. |
| 2. **Pummelling** | ~45s | Pairs, chest to chest, **on knees**. Each player has one arm inside (under the partner's armpit) and one outside. On the call, both "swim" the outside arm inside, and keep alternating. Chest up, back flat, **head to the side**, hips low, working continuously. |
| 3. **Wrist Wrestle** | ~45s | Pairs stand and each grips the **same wrist** on the other — right hand to right wrist, both of them, so the grips lock into a loop. Free hand behind the back. Each then tries to slap their partner with the hand being held. A point for each clean touch; reset and go again. Low base, feet moving, all grip and body position. |

Then, and only then, into the drill or game proper.

**Both games have hard rules.** Pummelling: no lifting, no takedowns, no twisting anyone's neck — it is an arm battle, not a wrestle to the floor. Wrist Wrestle: **shoulder or upper arm only** as a target, never the head or face, and it stops the moment either player lets go. Anyone who would rather not play the slapping game does a second round of pummelling instead — offer that up front rather than making someone ask for it.

- **Pair by size.** Not by friendship, and not whoever is standing nearest.
- **Never skip stages**, however short of time you are. If there isn't two minutes, there isn't contact — take it out of the session instead.
- **Anyone returning from injury, or new to the group, starts at stage 1** and joins the games only when a coach says so, regardless of what everyone else is doing.
- **Tackle cues get coached in the tackle work itself**, not rehearsed here. These two minutes are about getting warm, getting hands on someone, and arriving at the drill already switched on.
- **If contact groups rotate through a skill zone**, each group runs the two minutes as it arrives — the group that has been jogging in a handling game is no more contact-ready than the one that just walked off the touchline.

## Skills pyramid

Coaching builds up through four layers, each resting on the one below it — useful for placing any given piece of session content, and for judging whether a block or week is weighted appropriately for where the squad is:

1. **Individual skills** — catch, pass, throw, tackle — the technical building blocks every player needs, regardless of position.
2. **Small-group skills** — miss-pass, switch, a two- or three-player move (see `playbook.md`) — 2–3 players combining individual skills into a coordinated action.
3. **Team moves** — coordinated moves involving most or all of the team: a lineout strike move, a move off the scrum base (see `playbook.md`).
4. **Team strategy & tactics** — how those moves get selected and sequenced within a game plan: block themes (see `blocks.md`), kicking strategy, defensive system.

Early blocks and weeks weight toward the base of the pyramid; later ones build upward, once the layers below are solid enough to support them. This is the same whole–part–whole logic applied above within a single session, just at the scale of a block.
